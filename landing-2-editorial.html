Section One

The veto

There is a meeting that happens in every large organisation in the Kingdom, usually twice. In the first, the chief information officer explains that generative AI will take three weeks out of tender preparation, read forty years of drilling reports in an afternoon, and answer the procurement questions that currently consume a person a day. Everyone agrees. A budget is approved.

Marginal

The second meeting is the one that decides the programme. It is rarely on the same agenda, and it is almost never attended by the vendor.

In the second meeting, the chief information security officer asks one question: where does the text go?

It is not an obstructive question. It is the only question. A prompt is not a search query — a prompt is a document. To be useful it has to carry the thing that matters: the unpriced clause, the bid margin, the incident report, the salary band, the geological survey the company paid eleven million riyals to commission. The value of the answer is directly proportional to the sensitivity of the input. That is the uncomfortable arithmetic sitting at the centre of enterprise AI, and no amount of executive enthusiasm dissolves it.

The arithmetic

Useful answer ← sensitive input. There is no third variable. Every architecture that claims otherwise is quietly redacting something.

So the programme stalls — not because anybody doubts the technology, but because nobody can answer that question in a form that survives an audit. A vendor's assurance is not an answer. A data-processing agreement is not an answer. A regional endpoint is not an answer, because a region is a billing address, not a boundary.

A data-processing agreement is a promise. A network diagram is a fact.

The distinction this whole paper turns on

What the CISO wants is not reassurance. It is a diagram: this machine, in this building, on this VLAN, holding these weights, writing these log lines to this SIEM. Give an auditor that, and the veto disappears in an afternoon. Nothing else works, and everybody in the room already knows it.

Section Two

Three false resolutions

Organisations facing that veto reach for one of three answers. Each fails, and each fails in a way worth naming precisely, because the failures are what make the real resolution obvious.

One Ban it

The cleanest answer on paper and the worst in practice. Staff who are measured on output do not stop using a tool that doubles their output; they move it onto a personal phone and a consumer account. The ban does not stop the data leaving the organisation. It stops the organisation knowing that it left. Shadow AI is not a discipline problem — it is what a policy looks like when it is cheaper to break than to follow.

Two Redact it

Strip the names, the numbers, the clauses; send the husk to a public API. This works, in the narrow sense that nothing sensitive leaves. It also removes precisely the content that would have made the answer worth having. A model that cannot see the contract cannot review the contract. Redaction converts a strategic capability into a grammar checker, and the users notice within a fortnight.

Observed

Redaction pipelines are also where the leaks come from. Every one of them is a regex someone wrote on a Thursday.

Three Buy something called “private”

The most seductive of the three, because the word private is doing an enormous amount of work. A dedicated tenancy in a hyperscaler region is still a network path out of your estate; still keys held in somebody else's key-management service; still a control plane you do not operate; still a jurisdiction whose courts you did not choose; still a vendor who can change the terms of the deal on ninety days' notice, and has. It is a better promise than the public API. It is still a promise.

Private is a word about intent. Sovereign is a word about location.

Notice what all three have in common. They accept as fixed the one thing that is not fixed — that the model must run on somebody else's hardware. Remove that assumption and every one of the three problems evaporates at once. You do not need a redaction pipeline if the prompt never crosses the boundary. You do not need a policy against shadow AI if the sanctioned tool is the better one. You do not need to read the sub-processor list if there are no sub-processors.

Section Three

What changed

For several years that assumption was fixed, and the CISOs were right to veto. Two things changed, and they changed at roughly the same time.

Frontier-class capability stopped being something you can only rent. Open-weight models now land within touching distance of the closed frontier: Kimi K2 Instruct at a trillion parameters of mixture-of-experts, DeepSeek V3.1 at 671 billion, Qwen 3 at 235 billion, NVIDIA Nemotron at 120 billion. These are not distillations or toys. They are the same class of system — long-context, tool-using, genuinely good at reading a document nobody wants to read — and their weights can sit on a disk you own, in a building you can walk into.

Most customers do not buy a POD. They buy a slice of one: a typical workspace tenancy is eight H200s in Riyadh DC-1, which is enough to serve a few thousand people who all think the answer arrived instantly.

You are no longer choosing between the capability and the control, because the capability has stopped demanding that you give up the control. That is the entire argument of this paper. Everything after this point is engineering — which is to say, everything after this point is the part you are allowed to verify.

Section Four

What happens to a prompt

Argument is cheap; a trace is not. Here is what happens between a user pressing Enter in MagnaVERSE and the first token arriving back, on the private tier, in the default configuration.

  1. The keystroke

    The workspace is served from inside your estate. The browser resolves an internal name to a virtual IP on your own VLAN, and TLS terminates on hardware in the building. There is no step here where the request touches the public internet, because there is no route by which it could.

  2. The policy check

    Before a single token is generated, the request is evaluated against your policy set: who the user is, which sovereignty tier they selected, which connectors this workspace may touch, what this user's group is permitted to ask for. Denials happen here — in front of the model, not after it has already read the document.

  3. Retrieval

    If the question needs context, it is retrieved from your own index — the digital-twin context graph built across your SAP S/4HANA records, your Primavera P6 schedules, your Confluence spaces, your Jira projects and your mail. The index was built on your hardware and has never been anywhere else. Retrieval respects the same permissions the source systems do, so a user cannot read through the model what they could not read directly.

  4. Inference

    The prompt, the retrieved passages and the system policy are assembled and sent to eight H200s in Riyadh DC-1 — a tenancy inside a MagnaREACTOR POD, or a rack in your own building if that is the deployment you chose. The weights are on local NVMe. The KV cache lives in HBM and dies with the request.

  5. Tools

    If the answer requires an action — raise the ticket, pull the float on activity 4120, draft the reply — the agent calls it over MCP from inside the network, using the user's own credentials and the user's own permissions. Nothing is performed by a service account nobody can name.

  6. The answer

    Tokens stream back along the same path they came. Citations point at document IDs in your systems, so a sceptical reader can open the source rather than trust the summary.

  7. The record

    An audit record is written and shipped to your SIEM: identity, timestamp, tier, model, prompt hash, every document ID retrieved, every policy decision, every tool call, token counts. It is written for the auditor rather than for the dashboard, which is why it is boring and complete.

What left the building: nothing. That is not a marketing claim. It is a property of the network, and you can prove it with a packet capture.

The honest part: there is a public tier

MagnaVERSE can route to a public model, because sometimes the honest answer is that a task does not need sovereignty — rewriting a job advert, summarising a press release. Pretending otherwise would push people back to their phones, which is the failure mode in Section Two.

So the public tier exists, and it cannot be entered by accident. It requires an explicit confirmation naming what is about to leave, it is stamped visibly on the transcript, and it is in the audit log permanently. The point of a boundary is not that nothing ever crosses it. The point is that every crossing has a name, a time and a person attached.

Ask any AI vendor two questions. Where do the weights live, and where does the KV cache die?

If the second answer is “we don't publish that”, you have your answer
Section Five

The workspace

None of the above matters if nobody uses it. This is where most sovereign AI projects die: the architecture is impeccable and the interface is a research demo with a submit button, so the organisation buys a beautiful private cluster and the staff keep using the thing on their phones.

Design brief

Zero training. If a new user needs a session to learn the chat box, the security argument has already lost.

So MagnaVERSE looks like the tool everyone has already learned. A thread on the left, a conversation in the middle, a composer at the bottom; attachments, history, a model picker. That familiarity is deliberate and it is not a small thing — it is the mechanism by which a security decision becomes a product people actually adopt. The only visible difference is a residency indicator that tells you, on every answer, which tier and which model produced it.

Underneath, it does rather more than chat. There are eighty-plus pre-built agents across thirty-four verticals — tender comparison, HSE incident triage, schedule-delay analysis, invoice reconciliation — each one a starting point rather than a blank prompt. There is workflow automation, so the ones that earn their place stop being asked and start running. There are enterprise connectors: SAP S/4HANA, Primavera P6, Jira, Confluence and mail, reached over MCP from inside the network. And there is the digital-twin context graph, which is the part that makes answers specific — it knows that this purchase order belongs to that package on that project, and that the delay in one is the reason for the variation in the other.

Governance runs across all of it: policy enforcement in front of the model, full audit trails behind it, and more than a thousand validated use cases already documented so that the first question in a workshop is “which of these” rather than “what could we possibly do with this”.

Section Six

Where it runs

“Sovereign” is not one deployment. It is a question about who holds the boundary, and there are three defensible answers depending on what your regulator, your network and your power budget will allow.

One On-premises

Racks in your building, on your power, behind your firewall, under your change-control process. Magna installs and can operate it, but the boundary is drawn at your property line and nothing crosses it. This is the deployment for the customer whose regulator asks for a floor plan rather than a certificate — defence-adjacent manufacturing, critical infrastructure, anyone whose data classification has the word national in it.

Two Sovereign cloud

Magna-operated, in-country, single-tenant. You get the elasticity of a cloud and a written residency guarantee that the data stays inside the Kingdom, with 24/7 managed operations and someone else holding the pager at three in the morning. Five global regions exist for organisations that operate across borders and need each subsidiary's data to stay in its own jurisdiction rather than pooling in a convenient one.

Assurance

ISO 27001 aligned. Red-team audited. 24/7 managed operations. Data residency guaranteed in-country.

Three Edge

A plant, a rig, a remote site, a vessel. Compute that sits with the asset and keeps working when the link to headquarters does not, then reconciles when it returns. The interesting property of the edge deployment is not latency — it is that the operational data with the shortest half-life never has to travel at all.

All three run the same workspace, the same agents and the same governance model. Moving between them is a deployment decision, not a migration, and it does not change what your users see.

Section Seven

What we are asking for

Not your trust. Trust is what the other architecture requires, and it is exactly what your security team is right to withhold.

We are asking for two hours and your hardest question — the document you would never paste into a public model, the workflow that touches four systems and three approvals, the audit requirement you assume no vendor can meet. Bring the CISO. The walkthrough is designed for the person who says no, because that is the only person whose agreement changes anything.

The capability, without the data leaving. Everything else in this paper is a consequence of that one sentence.
