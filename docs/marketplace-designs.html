C

The Ledger

No cards at all. A sortable table where the scopes are the primary column, because in a sovereign deployment the question is rarely "what does this do" and almost always "what will this be allowed to touch".

Sorted by grant severity. shell:execute and credentials:write rank above read-only scopes, so the two apps worth arguing about sit near the top rather than wherever the alphabet put them.

Strength. The only one of the five you could hand to a security reviewer. Every app, every scope, one screen, sortable — and sorting by grant severity puts the risky ones where they belong.

Weakness. Charmless, and it does not scale to a browsable catalogue of hundreds. This is an admin console, not a store.

D

Split detail

Master–detail. The list stays put, the right pane carries everything: description, scopes, contributed tools, what it registers. No modal — the decision and the evidence share a screen.

E

Capability-first

Invert the noun. Do not list apps — list what your agent will be able to do, and note which plugin supplies it. In an agent OS, "what can it do for me" is closer to the real question than "what is installed".

Run shell commands

from Terminal · installed · every call approvable

Contributes no tools of its own — it drives the harness's own bash tool, which is why each command is gated and logged.

Strength. It is the only framing that makes the empty state useful. "Read your mail — needs a connector" tells you what to go and get; an app grid with no mail app just silently lacks one.

Weakness. It buries install and remove, which are still the actions people came for, and seven of nine apps contribute no tools — so most of the catalogue has nowhere to appear.

06

Which one to build

These are not equally good for what you actually have.

DirectionBest whenBreaks whenVerdict
A · ShelfA curated catalogue with many publishersNine first-party plugins — the hero slot flatters and goes stalePremature. Revisit when third parties publish.
B · Rail and gridDozens to hundreds of items, mixed kindsScopes will not fit on a cardBuild this as the shell. It already matches the five tabs you have.
C · LedgerAuditing what is allowed to touch whatBrowsing, discovery, anything charmingBuild this as a view inside B, not as the whole marketplace.
D · SplitDeciding on one app, carefullyComparing, or installing something you already knowBuild the detail pane — it replaces the confirm dialog's job.
E · CapabilityAnswering "what can my agent do"7 of 9 apps contribute no toolsKeep as a second tab. Its empty states are the best argument here.

The recommendation is a composite, not one of the five

B as the frame, D as the detail, C as a toggle, E as a tab. Rail and grid for browsing; clicking a card opens the split detail rather than a modal, because that is where scopes and contributed tools have room to be read before you agree to them; a density toggle turns the grid into the ledger table for anyone auditing; and a Capabilities tab answers the question the app list cannot.

A is the one to skip. It is the most familiar and the least honest — an editorial storefront implies a marketplace that does not exist yet.

One thing all five get wrong, and the current build gets wrong too

Every design above shows scopes as static text. But docs:write on an app that holds your documents and shell:execute on one that can run anything are not the same kind of claim, and nothing in these layouts says so. Ranking grants by severity — as C's sort does — is the smallest change that makes the permission real rather than decorative, and it should carry into whichever direction you choose.
